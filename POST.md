# Where the bands came from

For years, I thought it was completely normal to know where every band came from. Radio DJs had a lot to do with it. Instead of repeating the name of a band, they would say things like "the Liverpool quartet", "the boys from Manchester". Music magazines and reviews did the same. I grew used to hearing geography woven into music talk, so I absorbed it without noticing.

It also made me dream about places that, as a latinamerican kid, I was very sure I wouldn't be able to visit.

Later, after I emigrated and began meeting people from the UK, they would tell me where they were from and a band would immediately appear in my head. Someone would say, "Newcastle," and my mind would answer The Animals. Sometimes I said it out loud. It took me a while to realize that this was a particular trait of mine and not an entirely normal way to respond when meeting someone.

I tend to save it for the smaller places. I would not comment on Liverpool, London or Manchester, where the associations are too obvious. Manchester is a special case anyway. To me, it may be the ultimate city of late-1980s cultural impact. First came Joy Division and New Order, then The Smiths, and then Madchester: The Stone Roses, Happy Mondays and the rest of that strange, brief explosion.

There were other cities lodged in my musical imagination. I knew that The B-52's and R.E.M. came from the same Athens, Georgia. I knew about Seattle, the scene that seemed to swallow an entire generation. So much was written about how one place could produce so many important bands. Some people blamed the rain: the kids were stuck indoors or in bars, and their only escape was to make music. I have no idea whether that explanation was ever convincing, but I loved it.

That is how I developed a fascination with the origins of bands, especially with places that felt exotic to a child growing up in Latin America. They were remote and expensive to reach, places I was half-convinced I would know only through records, radio programmes and magazine articles.

Then, one day last September, I began wondering how many bands I could name from each British city. The answer was: quite a lot. I made a rough list, the one that eventually became the starting point for this project.

The list led to a question. Are some of these cities more important to British music than others?

London should be the obvious winner. It is one of Europe's largest cities and one of the most influential cities in the world. With that many people in one place, musicians have a better chance of finding like-minded collaborators, venues, audiences and an industry willing to pay attention. Liverpool has The Beatles, of course, but what happens after The Beatles? Does the city's musical importance survive once its biggest band is taken away?

Perhaps the interesting question is not which city produced the single largest act, but which cities have depth: several bands, across different periods, with an audience that extends far beyond the place where they began. And perhaps that depth can be measured in a way that goes beyond my own taste and the catalogue of hometowns I have been carrying around in my head since childhood.

That sounded like an interesting data problem.
